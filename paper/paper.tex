\begin{abstract}
The AKBC 2026 shared task asks a language model to recover complete object sets
closed-book. \systemname{} anchors each subject, samples several candidate
sets, and aggregates them per relation, reaching 0.513 macro F1. Sample count,
aggregation rule, and abstention thresholds each move the score by less than
the evaluation set's own 0.045 confidence interval; only the choice of model
does. The binding constraint is elementary: closed-book prediction cannot
recover an object that appears in no sample.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

How can knowledge stored in a language model be turned into a complete database
entry? A model may readily answer that the Dominican Republic borders Haiti,
yet the same question becomes considerably harder when the expected answer
contains nine neighboring countries, 80 award recipients, or no object at all.
The AKBC 2026 shared task isolates this problem: given a subject \(s\) and a
relation \(r\), a system must predict the complete set of object strings
\(\{o_1,\ldots,o_k\}\) \citep{akbc2026}.

Consider a country with three gold neighbors. Returning two correct ones yields
perfect precision and \(2/3\) recall, while adding three plausible but wrong
neighbors leaves recall unchanged and cuts precision to \(2/5\); inventing a
death city for a living person turns an otherwise perfect row into a complete
miss. The design question is therefore not whether the model knows a fact, but
when a further candidate should be admitted.

We answer it with description-first relation-wise self-consistency, which
identifies the exact subject, generates candidate sets through prompts exposing
different recall paths, and admits empty answers into aggregation rather than
gating generation beforehand. It reaches 0.513 macro F1 on validation.

The instructive result however is what did not move that number. Sample count is
flat from three to twenty passes, no aggregation rule beats a plain median, and
calibrating abstention thresholds on training traces reproduced the defaults
exactly. Only the model mattered. The reason is elementary: a correct area value
appears in the samples for 0.69 of rows under one eligible model and 0.43 under
another, and no rule selects a value never generated.

\section{Task}
\label{sec:task}

The dataset contains six relations and 477 training, 478 validation, and 475
test rows; we reserve validation for evaluation, and fit prompts and thresholds
on training data only. Cardinality varies sharply across relations. Area and
capacity carry exactly one numeric object, accepted within 5\% relative
tolerance. Death cities and stock exchanges are gold-empty on 39 and 36 of
their 100 validation rows, so returning nothing is often the correct answer.
Borders reach 14 neighbors and one award row has 610 recipients. String
predictions are matched after normalization and alias expansion, and the
official score is macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 over
subject--relation pairs.

The setting is closed-book: inference may use open-weight models totalling at
most 32 billion published parameters, but may not query the web, a retrieval
index, an external corpus, or a knowledge base, and fine-tuning is prohibited.
In-context examples, multi-step inference, and non-neural post-processing are
allowed \citep{akbc2026}. Our system therefore uses the training split only for
few-shot demonstrations and no external factual source.

\subsection{Common inference procedure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows one row of inference. All calls use one
open-weight model through an OpenAI-compatible chat endpoint. The model first
produces a short identity anchor describing entity type, scope, and likely
namesake confusions, then receives that anchor as explicitly uncertain context
and generates $n_r$ concise rationales and candidate sets. Each pass uses a
different seed, recall focus, and rotating leave-one-subject-out sample of
training demonstrations. Passes are independent by construction and issued
concurrently, with trace records ordered by request rather than completion so a
concurrent run reproduces a serial one exactly.

For relations that permit empty answers, the demonstration mix mirrors the
relation's own training empty rate---two of five for death cities, one of five
for borders---rather than being fixed in advance. An earlier version alternated
empty and non-empty examples, showing a 60\% empty rate for every relation and
biasing the model toward abstention on precisely the relations where abstention
is scored.

Candidate strings are parsed from the first valid JSON structure and normalized
with the official evaluator's rules. We additionally build relation-specific
alias maps from the nested aliases in the training split, rewriting an alias
only when all its training occurrences identify one canonical entity, before
support is counted so that equivalent surfaces reinforce rather than split a
vote. A response the parser cannot read is dropped from its row's aggregate
rather than counted as empty, which would convert a parser failure into a vote
for ``no objects''; this never occurred in 5{,}392 validation calls. All
prompts, responses, and aggregation decisions are retained in immutable per-row
traces.

\paragraph{Seven numeric samples, and a median.}
Both follow a training-only sweep rather than convention. Over the 100 training
area rows accuracy is flat between 0.53 and 0.58 for every count from 3 to 20
and for every estimator we tried, including mode-seeking, trimmed, and
order-of-magnitude-filtered variants. Twenty samples cost three times as much as
seven and bought nothing measurable; the median survives by default rather than
by merit.

\paragraph{Post-processing runs only on a non-empty set.}
This gate was the largest correction we made and we did not anticipate it.
Asking ``which neighbors are missing?'' is a leading question, and a model
asked for omissions supplies them whether or not any exist: in 12 of the 18
gold-empty validation rows every sample correctly returned no neighbor, after
which the completion pass invented Italy for Malta, Cuba and the United States
for the Bahamas, and three maritime neighbors for Australia. The verifier
accepted all of them. Across the split completion introduced 36 neighbors while
verification removed 22. An omission pass presupposes a set to have omissions
from; gating it raised border F1 from 0.796 to 0.859.

\paragraph{Awards are partitioned by era.}
A single answer may contain 610 recipients, so one undifferentiated prompt
invites both truncation and omission. Twenty passes cycle through five time
windows and a sixth focus organized by profession and recipient type. We apply
no binary verifier: an earlier one cut correct recipients from 96 to 56.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Models.}
We evaluate Gemma 4 31B-IT (30.7B published total parameters) and Gemma 3
27B-IT (27.4B) on complete splits, and screen Qwen3.5 27B on area alone. Every
relation in a run uses one model, so the inference-time total never exceeds
32B; quantization does not reduce it and mixture-of-experts models count by
published total.

\paragraph{Inference configuration.}
Inference runs through a hosted OpenAI-compatible gateway. Such gateways route
one model name across backends at precisions from fp4 to bf16, so we pin the
serving provider and bf16 precision and record the provider on every call;
otherwise a comparison confounds model identity with numeric precision. Runs
use temperature 0.6, top-$p$ 0.95, a 4{,}096-token cap, and five in-context
examples except for awards. Hidden reasoning is disabled explicitly: it is not
bounded by the generation cap and is billed at the completion rate, and one
100-row screen on a reasoning-tuned model emitted 3.87M such tokens. A complete
validation run costs 5{,}392 calls and about 80 minutes.

\paragraph{Selection and ablations.}
All thresholds are selected from complete leave-one-out training traces with
the official evaluator, and the locked configuration is then evaluated on the
validation rows. We do not tune thresholds, sample counts, aggregation rules,
or entity-specific rules against validation answers, and because the validation
gold is distributed with the task we additionally report training-split scores
as an independent estimate. The historical baseline is an earlier GPT-OSS-20B
prototype scoring 0.460, whose hard empty-answer gates and award verifier the
present system removes. Comparisons use a paired bootstrap over rows with
20{,}000 resamples.

On all 478 validation rows the system reaches macro precision 0.542, recall
0.578, and F1 0.513, against 0.460 for an earlier gated pipeline of ours on a
different model. The 95\% bootstrap interval is $[0.468, 0.557]$, and an
independent run on the training split scores 0.484.

Table~\ref{tab:per-relation} shows the two models are complementary rather than
ordered. Gemma 4 is better on every relation rewarding adherence to a precise
definition, reaching 0.989 on borders where one row separates it from a perfect
score, while Gemma 3 is better on the two relations rewarding raw numeric
recall. Only those two effects survive a paired bootstrap and they nearly
cancel: the overall $+0.022$ has interval $[-0.022, +0.067]$ and $p=0.33$. The
best system we can describe is one we may not submit, since pairing Gemma 3's
area with Gemma 4's other relations would score 0.544 but sum to 58.1B
parameters.

\paragraph{Tuning does not move the score; knowledge does.}
Three of our four controlled comparisons returned null results. Numeric sample
count is flat from 3 to 20 samples. No aggregation rule beats a plain median.
Sweeping the abstention thresholds on complete training traces reproduced the
uncalibrated defaults, changing death and exchange F1 by 0.000 each. Only the
model changed anything, and the changes it produced were relation-specific
rather than uniform.

The reason is visible in the candidate pools. For \texttt{hasArea} a correct
value appears in at least one sample for 0.69 of rows under Gemma 3 and 0.43
under Gemma 4, while the aggregates return 0.54 and 0.37; for
\texttt{hasCapacity} the ceiling is 0.19. Where the correct value is absent
from every sample no selection rule can recover it, and where it is present but
in the minority, agreement-based aggregation discards it because an incorrect
value dominates the distribution.

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error-analysis}

\paragraph{Abstention is too conservative, not too aggressive.}
On stock exchanges the system predicts empty on all 36 gold-empty rows with no
spurious answer at all, yet returns nothing on 26 rows that do have a listing;
death cities are correct on 33 of 39 gold-empty rows against 32 rows where a
recoverable city was suppressed. This is a negative result about our own design:
the prototype's hard pre-generation gate suppressed 28 of 61 non-empty death
rows and we replaced it with generation-first voting to recover them, yet the
replacement suppresses 32. The mechanism changed from a classifier to a vote but
the operating point did not, because the samples agree on silence---when the
model does not know, twenty passes independently return nothing, and no rule
over unanimous abstentions manufactures a city.

\paragraph{Parsing is not the limiting factor.}
Because our central claim is that correct objects are absent rather than
mishandled, we audited it: across 5{,}392 calls the parser never dropped a gold
alias present in an answer array. Of the incorrect rows, 58 of 63 for area, 71
of 78 for capacity and 38 of 48 for death contain no correct value anywhere in
any response, while aggregation discards a correct minority value in 15 further
rows, worth about 0.03 overall.

Stock exchanges are the exception. In 9 of 27 incorrect rows the model names the
correct exchange in its rationale and then returns an empty set because it
believes the listing has ended---Leclanché is ``previously listed on the SIX
Swiss Exchange, but delisted following its acquisition''. Given that judgement
it correctly obeys our instruction to return nothing for a company that is not
independently public, so reading the answer out of the rationale would mean
admitting its own negations. Here the ceiling is set by temporal belief rather
than by missing knowledge.

\paragraph{Remaining errors.}
Median numeric error is 3.2\%, so failures are misidentification rather than
noise: \emph{Mainland} returns 9{,}596{,}961, the area of China rather than of
the 540\,km$^2$ island. The identity anchor was introduced for exactly this and
does not eliminate it. Border recall is 0.998 once the omission pass is gated.
Awards average 68.0 predicted entities against a gold mean of 145.8, up from
15.2, but ten rows cannot be measured to better than $\pm0.11$.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

LAMA established that pretrained models hold retrievable factual associations
\citep{petroni2019language} and closed-book question answering that they can be
queried without document access \citep{roberts2020knowledge}. Both ask whether a
known object is recovered, not how many exist, which is what makes this task
hard: a probe scored on its top answer never has to decide that a country has
nine neighbors or that a living person has no city of death. Phrasing alone
moves probing results substantially \citep{elazar2021pararel}, so we vary
phrasing across passes rather than search for one best prompt. Retrieval would
supply exactly the long-tail facts parametric memory covers worst
\citep{lewis2020rag,mallen2023popqa}; forbidding it leaves prompt diversity and
repeated sampling as the only sources of variation.

The task has run annually with shifting resource rules
\citep{kalo2024lmkbc,kalo2025lmkbc}. The 2025 edition is closest to ours: it
fixed a single small model, so methods rather than models distinguished the
entries. The winner combined chain-of-thought prompting with relation-wise
self-consistency and train-tuned per-relation aggregation
\citep{albertroulhac2025rewise}; the runner-up generated an entity description
before extraction and decomposed high-cardinality relations
\citep{he2025selfrag}. We adopt both. Our contribution is not their invention
but a measurement of what they are worth once the model may vary, which a
fixed-model setting cannot reveal.

Self-consistency aggregates repeated samples by majority vote
\citep{wang2023selfconsistency}, but assumes one final answer; set prediction
instead votes over normalized candidates, where a value generated once in twenty
passes may still be correct. Chain-of-verification reduces hallucination through
separate checking questions \citep{dhuliawala2024cove}, though a closed-book
verifier consults the same parameters as the generator, so we verify only
borders. Finally, a third of death and exchange rows are gold-empty, making
abstention a first-class prediction as in unanswerable-question benchmarks
\citep{rajpurkar2018squad2} and calibrated selective prediction
\citep{kamath2020selective}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a description-first self-consistency pipeline for closed-book
knowledge base construction, sharing one orchestration pattern across relations
while assigning aggregation by cardinality. It scores 0.513 on validation.

The more useful result is where the remaining error lives. Varying sample count,
aggregation rule, abstention threshold, and model, only the last changes the
score, and its gains are relation-specific and mutually exclusive under a
parameter budget. An incorrect value that dominates the sample distribution
defeats agreement-based selection precisely when agreement is what one would
like to trust. Effort is therefore better spent on elicitation diversity than on
aggregation, and reporting a candidate-pool ceiling alongside the score would
make systems easier to compare.

\section*{Limitations}

The system cannot recover facts absent from the model's pretraining data,
which matters most for deaths, listings, and awards close to the July 2026
reference date. Reusing one model for border generation and verification may
reinforce rather than correct a misconception, and train-calibrated thresholds
may shift under distribution change. We therefore reserve the test split for a
single final evaluation and record every prompt, response, and configuration.
